      Sorn ศรณ์

      Khlong Toei, Bangkok · Thailand · Southern Thai

      Michelin status
      ★★★ MICHELIN Guide Thailand 2026 (three stars since the 2025 edition)
      Leadership
      Supaksorn “Ice” Jongsiri, chef-owner; Yodkwan “Yod” U-Pumpruk, co-founder and head chef
      Format
      One tasting menu, shared main course. No à la carte.
      Price
      Not published on the official site. Diners in 2025–26 report THB 6,500 before service and tax; The Star put the all-in figure at THB 7,700 (January 2025). Confirm when booking.
      Menu used here
      Classic Menu, official PDF created 2 September 2026. Seasonal Menu PDF (16 September 2026) also reproduced.
      Website
      sornfinesouthern.com

      Bangkok has long treated the cooking of the peninsula as the loud relation: hotter, sourer, yellower with turmeric, and shaped by Malay Muslims and Hokkien traders as much as by the capital. Sorn became the first Thai restaurant in the world to hold three Michelin stars by cooking that food without softening it.

      Why Sorn is in this atlas

      EditorialSorn publishes its full menu, in Thai and English, as a downloadable document. That alone makes it rare at this level. The menu is also a map: nearly every line names a river, a coast or a province, and the dishes it reworks come from three distinct communities of the South. It is the clearest case in the collection of a restaurant using a nation's top culinary honour to argue that one region's food deserves the same standing as the court cooking of the capital.

      Thailand, and which Thailand

      BackgroundOutside the country, “Thai food” usually means the cooking of the Central Plains: coconut curries, jasmine rice, the balanced sweet-sour-salty-hot palate refined in Bangkok and its royal kitchens. That is one tradition among several. The North eats sticky rice with chilli relishes and shares much with Shan and Burmese neighbours. The Northeast, Isan, is culturally Lao, built on sticky rice, fermented fish (pla ra) and grilled meats. The South runs down a narrow peninsula between the Andaman Sea and the Gulf of Thailand, and its food is dominated by the sea, by turmeric, and by curries without coconut milk that are sharper and hotter than anything in the centre.

      BackgroundTrade layered the whole country. Chillies arrived from the Americas through Portuguese and other merchants from the sixteenth century, then took over Thai cooking so thoroughly that it is hard to imagine it without them. Chinese migrants brought the wok, noodles and pork. In the South the layering is especially visible. The three southernmost provinces, Pattani, Yala and Narathiwat, are majority Malay Muslim and were once part of the Sultanate of Pattani, whose territory reached into what is now Kelantan in Malaysia; the Michelin Guide's own history of khao yam traces the dish to that Malay tradition. Phuket, on the Andaman side, has a Hokkien and Peranakan food culture that shares more with Penang than with Bangkok.

      BackgroundBangkok's current generation of chefs has turned this regional variety into a fine-dining subject. Sorn's ground is the South: its kitchen sources from the region's fourteen provinces, according to the MICHELIN Guide's 2025 inspector report.

      The restaurant

      Philosophy

      Official siteSorn's own statement is unusually modest for a three-star kitchen. Its headline is “Honouring the Past. Cooking for the Future”, and it says Southern Thai food does not need reinventing; what it needs is time, patience and care. The name is said to come from the Sanskrit “Sorana”, and the Michelin Guide glosses the related word as a refuge.

      DocumentedJongsiri did not train in a culinary school. He told the Michelin Guide that he learned to cook from his grandmother, and much of his team has no formal training either. Sorn opened in 2018, created by Jongsiri with Yodkwan U-Pumpruk and other cooks from Baan Ice, his family's Southern restaurant in Bangkok; all were natives of the South (Bangkok Post, 2019). The World's 50 Best puts the share of hyperlocal Southern ingredients at nearly 90 per cent.

      The geography of the menu

      Confirmed menuRead the Classic Menu as an atlas and both coasts appear. From the Andaman side come Phuket lobster, Andaman squid and the mineral water from Ranong used to steam the rice. From the Gulf side come Tapi River prawns: the Tapi drains Surat Thani province into the Gulf of Thailand. The rice salad is dressed with budu, the fermented fish sauce of the Malay south, and the closing dessert is a Phuket Hokkien sweet.

      DocumentedThe restaurant has long extended this to the fuel and water: in 2022 Jongsiri told The Nation that even the charcoal and the cooking water come from the South. The crabs for the signature course are delivered from Surat Thani every morning (MICHELIN Guide, 2020).

      Cultural context

      EditorialThree Southern communities are on this menu. The Malay Muslim deep south supplies khao yam, budu and the roti with green curry; the boat-building fishing culture of the same coast is referenced in a course named Gor Lae, after the painted kolae boats of Pattani and Narathiwat. Phuket's Hokkien Chinese supply Bi Co Moi. The Thai Buddhist fishing villages of the Gulf supply the crab, the prawns and the habit of eating everything with chilli paste. Sorn presents them as one regional cuisine, which is how Southerners tend to eat, though it is not how Thai national food culture has usually been told.

      Techniques

      Official siteThe site names charcoal cooking, clay pots, stone grinding, traditional rice steaming, and chilli pastes made in-house with pestle and mortar.

      DocumentedFor the crab course the kitchen pounds its chilli paste using three different techniques so that it clings to the crab without gelatine or any stabiliser (MICHELIN Guide, 2020). The rice is steamed over charcoal in a clay pot; the Michelin inspector was able to watch it being done from the first floor.

      People and producers

      Official siteSorn says its produce comes directly from farmers, fishermen and small communities across the South. It names none of them, and none is named on the menu. That is a real limit on what can be verified here.

      DocumentedEarlier reporting gives glimpses: a seaweed powder dried on the restaurant's roof and supplied by a Krabi researcher identified as Associate Professor Mantana (Bangkok Post, 2019), and Surat Thani crabs delivered at dawn (MICHELIN Guide, 2020). Both may no longer apply.

      The dining experience

      DocumentedSorn occupies a restored two-storey villa in a leafy lane off Sukhumvit 26. Each table is greeted by the chef, and staff ask every guest how much heat they want before explaining where each course comes from. The meal runs as a sequence of single plates, then turns into a shared Thai table with rice, condiments and side dishes, then ends with a dessert trolley (MICHELIN Guide inspector report, February 2025). The cellar holds more than 500 labels, according to the official site. In 2025 the inspector noted that online bookings opened at noon on the 1st, 21st and 25th of each month; check the current system before planning around it.

      What makes it worth three stars

      EditorialPlenty of restaurants refine regional food. Sorn's distinction is structural. It keeps the architecture of a Southern home meal, in which the rice and its accompaniments are the point and individual dishes exist to be eaten with rice, and it holds the heat at the level a Southerner would expect. Precision shows up in the places a diner would not think to look: rice steamed in water from a named province, chilli paste engineered to stay on a crab leg. The kitchen also changes slowly. The crab stick and the rice salad have been served since 2018, which suggests a restaurant more interested in perfecting a canon than in novelty.

      Five dishes, read closely

      Crab Stick กรรชูเปียงCourse 7, Classic Menu · served since opening
      A
      What it is

      Confirmed menuBlue crab, mud crab roe and chilli paste. DocumentedA single leg of crab, shell pointing up, its meat coated in roe and set in a small granite mortar holding a fiery yellow chilli dip, eaten in one or two bites like a lolly (The World's 50 Best). The crab is brought in alive, steamed and grilled; the meat and the roe are cooked at different temperatures before being brought together (The Star, 2025; Bangkok Post, 2019).

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      DocumentedThe source dish is khan chiang pu, the crab leg, which 50 Best calls the most fought-over part of the crab at any Thai table. Jongsiri has said the idea came from eating crab roe with chilli paste at a local restaurant and finding the roe overcooked (MICHELIN Guide, 2020). BackgroundCrab with a pounded chilli paste is ordinary seaside eating along the Gulf coast; this is the restaurant version of a fisherman's snack.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      DocumentedTwo kinds of crab, large males and roe-bearing females, arrive from Surat Thani on the Gulf coast every morning (MICHELIN Guide, 2020). Confirmed menuThe current Classic Menu specifies blue crab with mud crab roe; the Seasonal Menu switches to mangrove crab and adds the crab's tomalley, which suggests the dish follows what the coast is landing.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      DocumentedThe challenge was physical: a paste loose enough to taste fresh but firm enough to cling to the meat. The kitchen solved it with technique alone, pounding the chillies three different ways and using no gelatine or stabiliser (MICHELIN Guide, 2020).

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialThe dish shows Sorn's method in miniature. It takes the prized fragment of a shared, messy meal, isolates it, and serves it without lowering the heat. Anyone unfamiliar with the South should expect the chilli to be the point of the dish, with the sweetness of the crab there to carry it.

      The Sea Holds the Forest ข้าวยำCourse 10 · the only course shared by both menus
      A
      What it is

      Confirmed menuTurmeric rice, Southern herbs and budu sauce. DocumentedJasmine rice is steamed with turmeric, dried in the sun and fried in coconut oil, then tossed with pomelo, cucumber, green beans, lemongrass, torch ginger bud and bean sprouts, and dressed with Pattani budu sweetened with palm sugar and deepened with shrimp paste (The Star, January 2025). It has been on the menu since the restaurant opened.

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      BackgroundKhao yam is the Thai name for the dish Malay speakers call nasi kerabu, a breakfast and market staple of the Malay Muslim provinces. The Michelin Guide's 2018 history ties it to the Sultanate of Pattani, which once took in what is now Kelantan. In Pattani it is often made with rice tinted blue by butterfly pea flower and dressed with budu, the fermented fish sauce found in nearly every Southern Muslim kitchen. A 2024 BenarNews report from Pattani describes it as a cheap staple that some local cooks link to an eighteenth-century war, and notes that wealthier diners now pay thousands of baht for it as fine dining.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      BackgroundBudu is made by salting small fish, usually anchovy, and fermenting them for months. It is produced mainly in Pattani, Yala and Narathiwat, and across the border in Kelantan and Terengganu. The town of Sai Buri in Pattani is widely cited as the source of the best. Torch ginger bud (Etlingera elatior) and the other herbs are plants of the peninsula's forest and kitchen gardens.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialSorn makes two decisive changes. It swaps the blue rice of Pattani for turmeric yellow, the colour of the South's curries, and it crisps the grains so the salad has texture. Then it frames the dish as a landscape, a “forest” held by the sea, which turns a breakfast plate into a statement about where the South's flavours come from.

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialOf everything on the menu, this dish carries the most political weight. The deep south has lived with a separatist insurgency since 2004, and its Malay Muslim identity sits uneasily inside the national story. A three-star Bangkok restaurant made a Pattani breakfast its only unchanging course. For a newcomer, the unfamiliar note is budu: rounder, sweeter and more pungent than ordinary Thai fish sauce.

      Southern Jasmine Rice, “The Thai Way of Sharing”Course 11 · main course with condiments and sides
      A
      What it is

      Confirmed menuJasmine rice steamed over charcoal with mineral water from Ranong, served with five condiments and five side dishes for the table to share. DocumentedIt is cooked in a clay pot; The Star noted that the Thai test of good rice is that each grain stays separate (January 2025).

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      BackgroundIn a Thai meal rice is the meal, and everything else is kap khao, literally “with rice”, the term Sorn uses to head its sides. A Southern table usually adds a pounded chilli relish, fried or dried fish and raw vegetables to cool the heat. The menu's own Thai title for the course, กินอย่างไทย, means “eating the Thai way”.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      BackgroundRanong, on the Andaman coast near the Myanmar border, is known for its hot springs and mineral water. Confirmed menuThe menu calls the rice “Southern” but does not name a variety or a farm, a gap given how specific the rest of the menu is.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialTasting menus usually turn every dish into an individual plate. Sorn turns the meal back into a shared table halfway through, and puts its most technical effort into the plainest thing on it.

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialThe course reflects an understanding of Thai food in which the dishes serve the rice. Diners should pace themselves accordingly. The sides, stink beans with redtail catfish and roti with green curry among them, are meant to be eaten with spoonfuls of rice rather than on their own.

      A
      What it is

      Confirmed menuRoti with beef in a Southern green curry. DocumentedThe roti is crisp and flaky. The Star (2025) described the dish as a tribute to Muslim influence in the South, and the Michelin inspector called it both intriguing and powerful.

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      BackgroundRoti came to the Malay peninsula with Indian Muslim traders and is sold across the South at Muslim-run stalls, often for breakfast with curry. Beef is significant: many Chinese-Thai and some Thai Buddhist households avoid it, while Muslim Southern cooking uses it freely.

      C

      Confirmed menuThe menu says only “beef”. It does not name a cut, breed or farm, so the provenance cannot be checked.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialGreen curry is the most exported Thai dish there is. Serving it with a Muslim breakfast bread reminds the diner that “Thai” food in the South has always been a negotiation between communities.

      Bi Co Moi บีโกหมอยDessert, Classic Menu
      A
      What it is

      Confirmed menuBlack sticky rice, young coconut ice cream and cashew. DocumentedEarlier write-ups describe it as one of the menu's show-stoppers (The Star, 2025).

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      BackgroundBee-go-moi is the Phuket Hokkien name for black glutinous rice cooked to a porridge and topped with salted coconut milk. Phuket's cooking mixes Chinese, Malay and Thai influences, and the same dessert is sold in Penang as bee koh moy and across Malaysia as pulut hitam. Phuket's Hokkien community arrived largely with the tin-mining economy of the nineteenth century.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      BackgroundBlack glutinous rice is a whole-grain sticky rice whose bran turns the pudding deep purple. The Seasonal Menu's Thai name for its cashew course, กาหยู (kayu), is the Southern word for cashew, borrowed from Malay; it marks the nut as a Southern ingredient.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialThe salted coconut cream that normally sits on the warm porridge becomes a young-coconut ice cream, and the nuts add crunch. The flavour logic is kept, and only the temperatures change.

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialEnding a Southern Thai meal on a Hokkien street sweet recognises the Chinese layer of the peninsula, which the Thai national story often leaves out.

      What would you actually order?

      EditorialSorn serves a single menu, so the question becomes what to pay most attention to.

      Three most culturally interesting
      The Sea Holds the Forest, for the Pattani budu; the roti with green curry, for the Muslim South; Bi Co Moi, for Phuket's Hokkien layer.
      Most representative of the philosophy
      The Crab Stick: a regional snack refined with technique alone, at full heat.
      Most unfamiliar to an international diner

      A country house in a valley best known abroad for a First World War defeat, run by a self-taught cook who came into her partner's family business. It has become the most visible restaurant in Central Europe by cooking almost nothing that did not grow, graze or swim within reach of the kitchen.

      Why Hiša Franko is in this atlas

      EditorialSlovenia is the smallest country in this collection that holds a three-star restaurant, and Hiša Franko is the clearest example of a menu written by a landscape. Kobarid sits where Alpine pasture, Friulian lowland and Adriatic air meet, and the menu draws on each of them. It also lets us test a hard case for this atlas: a restaurant that changes its dishes constantly and does not publish them.

      Slovenia, a country at four crossroads

      BackgroundSlovenia is small enough to drive across in three hours, yet it touches four culinary worlds. The Alps in the north and west bring dairy, cured pork, buckwheat and barley. The Pannonian plain in the east, towards Hungary, brings pumpkin-seed oil, paprika and layered pastries. The Mediterranean coast and the Karst bring olive oil, air-dried ham, wine and the Venetian habit of polenta. The Dinaric south leans towards the Balkans. The national tourist board divides the country into two dozen gastronomic regions, a number that says more about local pride than about any neat boundary.

      BackgroundHistory added more layers. Centuries under the Habsburgs left strudel, dumplings and a Central European pastry tradition. The western edge, including the Soča Valley, was long tied to Friuli and Venice; jota, a sauerkraut and bean soup, is eaten on both sides of the Italian border. Seventy years in Yugoslavia brought grilled meats and burek to every town. Kobarid itself is the Caporetto of Italian history, the site of the 1917 breakthrough on the Isonzo front, and the valley still carries the memory of that front line.

      BackgroundThe MICHELIN Guide arrived only in 2020. Hiša Franko received two stars that year and a third in 2023, and the 2026 guide lists 74 Slovenian restaurants and 13 stars in total. Ana Roš, who has no formal culinary training, became the face of that change after the Netflix series Chef's Table featured her in 2016 and The World's 50 Best named her Best Female Chef in 2017.

      The restaurant

      Philosophy

      Official siteThe restaurant calls its cooking expressive, intense and unorthodox, and sums up its approach in two words: follow nature. It says every ingredient has a name, a face and a place. Each spring it writes a new menu that tells a different story about the valley; the core idea holds for the year while individual dishes keep changing with what is available. The 2026 menu is called Euphorest, a blend of euphoria and forest. In announcing it, Roš described stepping into the forest as the moment “the weight lifts”.

      The geography of its food

      BackgroundKobarid stands in the upper Soča Valley, the Isonzo of Italian maps, under the Julian Alps and a few kilometres from the Italian border. The river is famous for its turquoise water and its trout. Mediterranean and Central European air masses meet here, and the high pastures above Tolmin have produced cheese for centuries. Official siteThe kitchen describes itself as fiercely local. Its stated sources are a single forager who collects wild herbs, mushrooms and mountain plants each morning; a local fishing family whose waters were the last refuge of the native marble trout; high-mountain dairies; and farmers who raise the protected Drežnica goat, with lamb from the same community when goat is not available. DocumentedThe New York Times has reported that nearly every ingredient comes from within 50 kilometres (as cited by Wikipedia).

      Official siteSeafood is a notable exception. The site says it is becoming rare on the menu because the health of the seas no longer allows certainty. A northern Adriatic cuttlefish still appears in the autumn 2026 menu documented below.

      Cultural context

      DocumentedHiša Franko means “Franko's house”. Franko Kramar turned the property into a restaurant and guesthouse in the 1970s. It belonged to the family of Valter Kramar, Roš's partner at the time, and Roš became the cook. EditorialThe restaurant does not reconstruct Slovenian classics; you will not find a textbook jota or štruklji on the Euphorest menu. It takes the valley's raw materials and its peasant logic — cheese aged to survive winter, potatoes, barley in place of rice, honey and wax — and recombines them with techniques gathered by an international kitchen team. In that sense it challenges the idea that a national cuisine must be a list of dishes.

      Techniques

      DocumentedThe autumn 2026 menu shows baking in a crust of hay, ageing trout for two days before grilling it, hand-rolling pasta, toasting and creaming barley like a risotto, and infusing ice cream with beeswax. The kitchen makes its own cola. The non-alcoholic pairing is built from house juices, kombucha, infusions and ferments, including tomato water infused with hay (Reporter Gourmet, October 2026). Hiša Franko is also known for ageing the local Tolminc cheese; one long-time visitor described aged Tolminc taking on several distinct personalities (WNF Diary, updated September 2026).

      People and producers

      DocumentedNamed and unnamed suppliers documented in 2026: the trout tank outside the restaurant, fed by the adjoining stream; potatoes grown to Roš's specification by a nearby farm; beeswax from a local producer; Istrian olive oil from the head chef's neighbour; the Orto Felice kitchen garden; bread from Pekarna Ana, Roš's own bakery; and two biodynamic farmers from a Ljubljana market (Reporter Gourmet, October 2026). BackgroundThe valley's largest Tolminc maker is the Planika dairy in Kobarid, which produces about 100 tonnes a year (RTV Slovenija). This guide has not confirmed whether Hiša Franko buys from Planika or from mountain dairies directly.

      The dining experience

      DocumentedLunch begins on a green terrace with cocktails and brioches filled with Tolmin cheese before guests move inside (Plava kamenica, June 2026). One autumn 2026 account describes the first bite, a small wrap of seasonal vegetables and herbs, arriving on stones from the Soča under a veil of mist meant to evoke the river at dawn (Fab Foodie Swede, October 2026). Official siteThe house asks guests to arrive on time, accepts children over twelve, and cannot cater for dairy-free or vegan diets. That rule alone shows how central milk is to the menu. There are guest rooms, so many diners stay the night.

      What makes it worth three stars

      EditorialMichelin's own summary stresses Roš's personality and flavours with no half measures. The more interesting achievement is coherence under constant change. The dishes on the menu in June were not the dishes on the menu in October, yet both sets read unmistakably as the Soča Valley: dairy and acidity, wild green bitterness, smoke, river fish. Few kitchens at this level keep so much of the menu provisional while keeping its identity fixed.

      Five dishes, read closely

      Brioches with Tolmin cheeseFirst bite · and the cheese that runs through the whole menu
      A
      What it is

      DocumentedSmall brioches stuffed with Tolminc, served on the terrace with the welcome drinks (Reporter Gourmet; Plava kamenica). Skuta, the soft cheese made from whey, turns up later in the cream puff and the butterfly pasta.

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      BackgroundTolminc is a hard cow's-milk cheese named after the town of Tolmin, 15 kilometres down the valley. It was historically made in summer dairies on the mountain pastures of the Julian Alps, where milk had to become cheese to survive the trip down. The European Union registered Tolminc as a protected designation of origin in 2012. The mountain dairies have dwindled: according to a Google Arts & Culture feature, 68 were still working in the 1930s and fewer than 20 survive.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      BackgroundUnder the protected designation, only milk from cows grazing the defined Tolmin area may be used, and the pastures are mainly stocked with Brown Swiss cattle. The Planika dairy in Kobarid is the largest producer. Official siteHiša Franko says it works with high-mountain dairies but does not name them.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialPutting the valley's protected cheese into an enriched French dough as the first thing a guest eats is a statement of method: local material, borrowed technique. The restaurant's reputation for ageing Tolminc in-house, documented over several years, suggests it treats the cheese as a seasoning more than as a course.

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialAlpine dairying is a declining way of life, and Tolminc is its last commercial expression in this valley. A three-star kitchen using it from the first bite is economic support as much as gastronomy.

      Potato and hayItem 7 · one of two house classics
      A
      What it is

      DocumentedSmall potatoes baked in a crust of hay, served with sour cream flavoured with fenugreek and honey (Reporter Gourmet, October 2026). Another autumn visitor was served a 55-gram potato with cream flavoured with toasted yeast and honey, and a salty crust to crumble over it (Fab Foodie Swede, October 2026).

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      BackgroundHay is the backbone of Alpine farming: it feeds the cattle through winter, and Slovenia's free-standing wooden hayracks, the kozolec, are among the country's most recognisable rural structures. Cooking or smoking food in hay is an old Alpine technique that perfumes food with the scent of dried grass and flowers. The potato itself is the peasant staple of the mountains.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      DocumentedA nearby farm grows the potatoes to Roš's specification. When the farmer quoted “one euro”, Roš assumed it meant a kilo; it turned out to mean one potato (Reporter Gourmet).

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialThe humblest crop of the valley is grown and costed like a luxury ingredient and given the same attention a classical kitchen would give a truffle. The hay crust is functional: it seasons and steams at once.

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialThe dish asks the diner to taste the farming calendar. Hay is last summer's meadow; the potato is this autumn's harvest; the sour cream is the dairy that hay makes possible.

      Trota la reginaItem 12 · river fish
      A
      What it is

      DocumentedTrout from the tank outside the restaurant, aged for two days and grilled, served with lentils in several textures, herbs and flowers including Malabar spinach and blue borage, and a reduction of vinegar and butter (Reporter Gourmet, October 2026).

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      BackgroundThe Soča's native fish is the marble trout (Salmo marmoratus), a large predatory trout of the rivers that drain into the Adriatic. Decades of stocking with non-native brown trout led to widespread hybridisation, and genetic surveys found only a handful of pure populations surviving in remote headwater streams of the Soča basin. Local anglers' societies used those fish to restock the river. Fly fishing for these trout is now a pillar of valley tourism.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      Official siteHiša Franko says its trout comes from a local fishing family who look after the waters where the marble trout was found again, deep in the valley's gorges, and who help protect it. DocumentedThe October 2026 account places the fish in a tank fed by the adjoining stream. Neither source says which species is served; this guide has not been able to confirm whether it is marble trout, brown trout or a farmed hybrid.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialDry-ageing fish for a couple of days to concentrate flavour and firm the flesh is a recent technique borrowed from contemporary seafood kitchens; it is unusual for a freshwater fish. The lentils and the butter-vinegar sauce are Central European in spirit.

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialOf the restaurant's stated suppliers, this one ties the menu most directly to a conservation story. It also leaves the most important question unanswered: which trout are we eating?

      BeeswaxItem 15 · final dessert
      A
      What it is

      DocumentedAn ice cream infused with wax from a local beekeeper, served with a poppy-seed sauce (Reporter Gourmet, October 2026; Plava kamenica, June 2026).

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      BackgroundSlovenia is a nation of beekeepers. The Carniolan honeybee (Apis mellifera carnica) is native to the country, and it is the only bee Slovenian law allows beekeepers to keep. In 1770 Empress Maria Theresa appointed Anton Janša, from Upper Carniola, as the first teacher of beekeeping in the Habsburg lands. At Slovenia's initiative the United Nations declared his birthday, 20 May, World Bee Day. Painted panels on the fronts of beehives are a celebrated folk art. Poppy seed belongs to the pastry tradition of Central Europe.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      DocumentedThe wax comes from a local producer who is not named in the documented sources.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialWax is the part of the hive normally thrown away or turned into candles. Infused into dairy, it carries the resinous, floral smell of the comb without the sweetness of honey. That is a modern pastry idea built on the country's most traditional rural craft.

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialThe meal ends on the one agricultural tradition Slovenia has made an international cause. The dessert tastes of the country's meadows as surely as the hay potato does.

      The name means “claypot yeast”, after the ferment women in Thalía Barrios García's mountain village use to bake bread in clay pots. Her restaurant sorts its menu by the social life of each dish: everyday food, food that is disappearing, and food made only for weddings, saints' days and funerals.

      Why Levadura de Olla is in this atlas

      EditorialMexico's two two-star restaurants are both in the capital. Levadura de Olla was kept as an exception to this guide's two-star floor because no other starred kitchen in the country builds its menu as ethnography. Its sections tell you how a dish is used in community life before they tell you what is in it, and much of what it serves comes from one village of about 3,000 people in the Sierra Sur. Of the three restaurants investigated so far, it is the only one that publishes its whole menu with prices on its own website.

      Mexico, and Oaxaca within it

      BackgroundMaize was domesticated in southern Mexico thousands of years ago, and Mexican cooking still runs on it: tortillas, tamales, atoles, and moles thickened with masa. Masa is maize dough made by nixtamalisation, cooking the grain with lime to loosen the hull and improve its nutritional value. Beans, squash and chillies grow alongside the corn in the milpa, the shared field, together with the wild greens known as quelites. Spain brought pork, chicken, cattle, dairy, wheat and spices from its Asian trade, and much of what the world calls Mexican cooking is the result of combining them. In 2010 UNESCO inscribed traditional Mexican cuisine on its list of intangible cultural heritage.

      BackgroundRegional difference is enormous. The north eats wheat flour tortillas and beef; Yucatán cooks with Maya spice pastes and achiote; Puebla built mole poblano. Oaxaca, in the south, is the most diverse of all. The state has eight official regions and more Indigenous languages than any other in Mexico, including Zapotec, Mixtec, Mixe and Chatino. Its markets sell grasshoppers, dozens of chillies found nowhere else, and the moles that have made the capital a destination. The tourist phrase “land of the seven moles” flattens this: every village has its own version.

      BackgroundContemporary Mexican fine dining was long centred on Mexico City, where Pujol and Quintonil hold two stars each. Michelin's arrival in 2024 recognised Oaxaca separately, and Levadura de Olla was among its first starred restaurants. Its chef also won the guide's national Young Chef Award that year.

      The restaurant

      Philosophy

      Official siteThe menu opens with a principle: every dish has the same value and none is less important, because each technique and ingredient has its own history. It adds that, as in Oaxaca's villages, no good meal exists without a real tortilla, a hot salsa and well-seasoned beans. The restaurant describes itself as an “infinite library” of Oaxacan food, built by researching the markets and cooking habits of remote villages.

      DocumentedBarrios García has said that being born a woman in San Mateo meant being expected to learn to cook, and that meat is scarce there because of the village's location and the lack of refrigeration; vegetables lead most of her dishes for that reason (Culinary Backstreets). She studied gastronomy at university in Oaxaca and has said the training felt too Westernised compared with the cooking she grew up with (MexicoTrip).

      The geography of its food

      BackgroundSan Mateo Yucutindoó is a mountain municipality in the Sola de Vega district of the Sierra Sur, the range between the Central Valleys and the Pacific coast, with a population of 3,144 in the 2020 census. Official siteThe restaurant says the village's fields remain largely free of pesticides, and that its native crops and wild harvests are watered only by rain. In the community of Pueblo Viejo the team keeps its own milpa with maize, several kinds of bean, squash, nopal cactus and the ixtle maguey used for rope. Beyond the village, the menu reaches into most of Oaxaca's regions: Pacific fish, quelites from the Mixteca, drinks from the Isthmus and the Sierra Norte.

      Cultural context

      Official siteThe research pages are a record of village food life: Señora Celedonia serving mole de fiesta at a celebration; tamales reheated on the comal the day after a fiesta; the technique of trimming and soaking corn husks so the dough can be spread thinly. EditorialThis is a restaurant preserving an older tradition, and in doing so it challenges the idea that Oaxacan food means the moles of the Central Valleys. Its best-known mole has no chocolate and no seeds.

      Techniques

      Confirmed menuThe menu names comal cooking, charring (tatemado) of chillies, moles thickened with masa or cracked corn, dried pozole, confit, and fermentation in the house tepaches. River-stone molcajetes (mortars) are listed among the restaurant's tools. DocumentedWriters who have eaten here note that the seasoning revolves around smoke and an aromatic, gentle spiciness (Culinary Backstreets). Barrios García's second restaurant nearby, La Cocina de Humo, cooks round a comal at shared tables with a fixed menu.

      People and producers

      Official siteThis is the most transparent sourcing in the pilot. The restaurant names Señora Sixta Sarmiento of San Mateo, who makes 40-centimetre tostadas from blue maize from her own milpa for the restaurant; Señora Celedonia, who grows three local maize varieties, magueyano, olotón and tatanano; and Señora Rosa Sarmiento, who bakes the village's pan del bueno. The drinks list credits each maker: José Luis Toledo Morales for taberna palm wine from Unión Hidalgo; Rosalba Aquino Bautista for pozontle from the Sierra Norte; Hermilo Ayala Coronel for ticunchi from the Mixteca; Lesly Yesenia Cruz González and Javier Meza López for kalanca; Juan Carlos Hernández Cruz for pulque from Santiago Ixtaltepec.

      The dining experience

      DocumentedThe restaurant fills the courtyard of a colonial house on García Vigil. Visitors pass a “tomato room”, a table laid with hundreds of Oaxacan tomatoes of every colour and shape (Atlas Obscura, 2025). Food arrives on earthenware, and the drinks list leans on Indigenous ferments and mezcals from named agave species (TripAdvisor reviews, 2026). Official siteTables are booked by seating: 4pm and 7pm on Tuesdays, and 1pm, 4pm and 7pm Wednesday to Saturday.

      What makes it worth a star

      EditorialThe inspectors' own note praises the shaded courtyard and a grilled squash with white pipián and spearmint. The deeper achievement is editorial: a menu that teaches the diner how food functions in a community, with dishes priced in the hundreds of pesos. Technical polish is less obvious here than at Sorn or Hiša Franko, and some diners say so. One August 2026 review found the tasting menu educational but below what it expected of a starred kitchen. That is the trade-off of cooking village food without dressing it up.

      Five dishes, read closely

      Mole de fiesta tradicional de San Mateo YucutindoóCeremonial · MXN 360
      A
      What it is

      Confirmed menuA red mole of ancho and guajillo chillies and spices, thickened with masa, served with a chicken thigh and dried pozole maize cooked with hoja santa. The menu warns that it is slightly spicy and contains no seeds and no chocolate.

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      Official siteIn San Mateo this mole is served with chicken at special celebrations and ceremonies, always with the dried pozole. The research pages show Señora Celedonia serving it at a village fiesta. BackgroundMole is a feast food across Mexico, cooked in quantity by the families who sponsor a saint's day or a wedding. The well-known Oaxacan moles of the Central Valleys, mole negro above all, use dozens of ingredients including seeds, nuts, dried fruit and chocolate.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      BackgroundAncho is dried poblano; guajillo is dried mirasol. Both are trade chillies sold throughout Mexico. Pozole maize is a large-kernelled white corn. Hoja santa (Piper auritum) is a broad, anise-scented leaf used across southern Mexico. Thickening the mole with masa makes it a maize dish at heart.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialAs far as can be told, this is presented as the village version, without improvement. Its interpretation is the act of placing it on a starred menu under a heading that explains its social role.

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialA diner expecting Oaxacan mole to be dark, sweet and chocolate-rich will meet a lean, brick-red sauce. The surprise is the lesson: there are as many moles as there are villages, and the one sold to tourists is only one of them.

      Tamal de barbacollitaSafeguard · MXN 285
      A
      What it is

      Confirmed menuA tamal of cracked maize (maíz quebrajado) with pork and chicken in an adobo of puya and guajillo chillies and avocado leaf.

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      Official siteIt sits in the “safeguard” section: dishes little known outside their region of origin. The research pages describe San Mateo's distinctive tamal technique. The base of the dried corn husk (totomoxtle) is cut off and the husks soaked, so they soften and lose their small prickles; the dough is then spread in a thin layer. After a fiesta the leftover tamales are reheated on the comal for the next day's lunch. BackgroundThe tamal is among the oldest foods of Mesoamerica, and its forms vary from village to village.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      BackgroundCracked maize gives a coarser, grainier tamal than smooth masa. The leaves of native Mexican avocado trees carry an anise-like aroma and are a classic seasoning for barbacoa, beans and tamales in southern and central Mexico.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialThe kitchen preserves the dish and documents it. Its contribution is the classification: the recipe itself is left as San Mateo cooks it.

      E
      Why it matters

      Editorial“Safeguard” is a strong word. It tells the diner that the dish is at risk, and that ordering it is a small act of keeping it alive.

      Tamal de requesón, flor de calabaza, mole negro y coloraditoCreative · MXN 315
      A
      What it is

      Confirmed menuA tamal of maize dough mixed with requesón (fresh curd cheese), butter and spices, served with two moles, negro and coloradito, a dusting of dried cheese, coriander and a squash blossom. DocumentedThe Michelin inspectors cite a version of this tamal and note that Barrios García learned her tamales from her grandmother; Atlas Obscura's writer called it the most delicious thing on the menu (2025).

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      BackgroundMole negro and coloradito are two of Oaxaca's canonical festive moles, the first dark with burnt chillies and chocolate, the second brick-red and sweeter. Requesón and butter are colonial additions to a pre-Hispanic form.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      Official siteThe squash blossoms are male flowers: the restaurant's research notes explain that growers pick the males for eating and leave the females in the milpa to set squash, and that the flowers cannot be kept for more than a day.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialTwo festive moles on a single meatless tamal is a chef's composition. In a village they would belong to different occasions. It is filed under “creative” for that reason, and the restaurant is careful to say so.

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialIt shows the line the menu draws between documenting a tradition and departing from it, and it lets a newcomer taste the two moles side by side.

      Jitomates nativos oaxaqueñosCreative · MXN 370
      A
      What it is

      Confirmed menuAn assortment of Oaxacan native tomatoes on beetroot purée, with a fruit vinaigrette and quelites. DocumentedThe World's 50 Best calls it the chef's signature dish, and visitors pass a room laid with hundreds of native tomatoes on the way in (Atlas Obscura).

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      BackgroundThe tomato was domesticated in Mesoamerica; the English word comes from Nahuatl tomatl. Mexico's markets still sell landrace tomatoes in shapes and colours that never reached supermarkets elsewhere.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      Official siteThe menu says only “native Oaxacan”. The restaurant's stated sources are the rain-fed fields of San Mateo and markets across the state; individual growers are not named for this dish.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialA plate of raw tomatoes works almost as a seed-bank display. The cooking is minimal so that the varieties can be compared.

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialDiners from elsewhere think of the tomato as Italian. This dish returns it to its home and shows how much diversity was lost when a few varieties went global.

      NicuatoleDessert · MXN 185
      A
      What it is

      Confirmed menuA maize and burnt-milk dessert served with a granita of red prickly pear from San Martín Lachilá.

      B
      Cultural and historical significance

      BackgroundNicuatole is a set maize pudding associated with the Zapotec people and widely described as pre-Hispanic in origin. In its basic form it is maize, water, cinnamon and sugar or piloncillo, often streaked with red from cochineal (México Desconocido). In July 2024 Oaxaca's state congress declared it part of the state's intangible cultural heritage (Prensa Latina). San Agustín Yatareni holds an annual nicuatole and maize fair.

      C
      Ingredient provenance

      Confirmed menuThe prickly pear is credited to San Martín Lachilá, a village in the Central Valleys. BackgroundBurnt milk, leche quemada, is a famous Oaxacan ice-cream flavour; milk itself arrived with the Spanish.

      D
      The chef's interpretation

      EditorialThe kitchen pairs a pre-Hispanic maize sweet with the caramelised dairy of the colonial period and a cactus fruit. Three eras of Oaxacan food end up on one plate.

      E
      Why it matters

      EditorialIt is the clearest example of maize as sweet food, which most visitors never encounter, and a dessert the state now treats as heritage.

      The stone in the kitchen

      Sorn · Levadura de Olla

      EditorialBoth restaurants make the grinding stone visible. Sorn pounds its chilli pastes in pestle and mortar, using three distinct techniques for one dish, and serves its crab course in a small granite mortar. Levadura de Olla lists river-stone molcajetes among its tools. In both cuisines the texture of a sauce depends on stone. A blender shears ingredients, while a mortar crushes them and releases oils, which is why both kitchens keep doing it by hand.

      The grain is the meal

      Sorn · Levadura de Olla · Hiša Franko

      EditorialSorn heads its sides “with rice” and steams that rice in water from a named province. Levadura de Olla's menu begins by saying that no meal exists without a real tortilla. Hiša Franko cooks barley like risotto because rice will not grow in Slovenia. Three kitchens on three continents treat the staple grain as the thing every other dish exists to accompany, even at the luxury end of dining.

      Ferments from the margins

      Sorn · Levadura de Olla · Hiša Franko

      EditorialBudu comes from the Malay Muslim border provinces of Thailand. Taberna palm wine, pulque and ticunchi come from the Isthmus and the Mixteca, and each is credited to a named maker. Hiša Franko's non-alcoholic pairing is built on house ferments and kombucha. In each case fermentation carries the identity of a community that sits at the edge of the national food story, and the restaurant brings it to the centre.

      Taught by grandmothers, or by nobody

      Sorn · Levadura de Olla · Hiša Franko

      EditorialJongsiri has said he learned from his grandmother and never went to culinary school. Roš is self-taught. Barrios García did study gastronomy, and found it too Westernised for the food she grew up with; the inspectors credit her tamales to her grandmother. Two of three top-rated chefs here learned outside the classical system, and the third had to unlearn part of it.

      Rivers as provenance

      Sorn · Hiša Franko

      EditorialSorn's menu names the Tapi River on its first line. Hiša Franko's trout comes from a tank fed by a Soča tributary. Both use a river as a guarantee of place in the way wine uses a vineyard. In the Soča's case the guarantee is fragile: its native trout was nearly lost to decades of stocking with non-native fish.

      What fills the role of dairy

      Sorn · Hiša Franko · Levadura de Olla

      EditorialHiša Franko cannot serve a dairy-free guest; milk runs through the whole menu. Levadura de Olla uses requesón, quesillo and burnt milk, all colonial arrivals layered onto a maize base. Sorn's richness comes from coconut: milk, cream, meat and shoots appear in nearly every section. Each kitchen's fat says something about its landscape and its history of contact.

      What these menus reveal

      A provisional reading, written after the first three investigations. It will be rewritten when all fifteen are complete.

      The most striking thing about these three menus is how little they rely on the conventions that once defined the Michelin restaurant. None is French or French-derived. Only one is a classic tasting menu in the European sense, and even that one, Hiša Franko, refuses to fix its dishes long enough to print them. Sorn breaks its meal halfway through to become a shared Thai table. Levadura de Olla publishes every dish with its price and arranges them by when a village would eat them.

      What they share is a claim about scale. Each argues that the meaningful unit of a cuisine is smaller than the nation: a peninsula and its fourteen provinces, a valley under the Julian Alps, a village of three thousand people in the Sierra Sur. National cuisines, in this view, are federations, and the most interesting cooking happens where the parts meet: Malay and Thai, Alpine and Friulian, Mixtec and Spanish.

      They also share an attitude to the past. None of the three chefs presents tradition as something to be modernised out of. Sorn says Southern food does not need reinventing. Levadura de Olla files its own inventions under a separate heading so they cannot be mistaken for heritage. Hiša Franko uses invention to express a place, and its techniques are borrowed while its materials stay local. In each case the guide's highest recognition is being used to protect a way of eating that the market might otherwise erase.

      The open question is transparency. The restaurant with the fewest stars publishes the most: every dish, every price, the names of the women who make its tostadas. One of the two three-star restaurants here publishes no dishes at all. For a reader who wants to know what they are eating and where it came from, that gap matters, and it is one this atlas will keep measuring.

      Method and sources

      Selection

      Fifteen restaurants in fifteen countries, chosen for menus that are culturally specific, researchable and documented. The floor is two Michelin stars; three one-star restaurants are kept as exceptions because nothing else in their countries offers the same subject. Michelin publishes no restaurant guide for Africa or India, so Wolfgat (South Africa) and Masque (India) appear as clearly labelled unstarred bonus entries. Saudi Arabia's first guide (2026) awarded no stars, and no Emirati or Qatari kitchen holds one, so the Middle East is represented by Türkiye and by a Syrian kitchen in Dubai.

      Verification

      Star counts were checked against Michelin's own pages and press releases, national government or tourist-board releases, and national press. Several restaurant-aggregator sites contradicted Michelin on star counts and were not used for status. Menus were taken from restaurants' own websites wherever they exist; the date shown is the file's creation date or the page's last-modified date. Where a restaurant does not publish dishes, the most recent dated account is used and labelled.

      Labels

      Every factual paragraph carries a label showing its basis: confirmed on the current official menu or site; documented by a dated review or interview; background from history, ecology or culture; or editorial interpretation. Quotations are kept short and attributed. Nothing has been invented to fill a gap; where something could not be confirmed, the text says so.
